# The Central Dogma and the Genetic Code

The central dogma describes the flow of genetic information: DNA -> RNA -> protein, via
replication, transcription, and translation.

## DNA structure

DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel strands. The backbone is sugar (deoxyribose)
and phosphate; the rungs are paired nitrogenous bases. Adenine (A) pairs with thymine
(T) via two hydrogen bonds; guanine (G) pairs with cytosine (C) via three hydrogen
bonds. Strands run 5' to 3' and 3' to 5'.

## Replication (semiconservative)

DNA replication is semiconservative: each new double helix has one parental and one new
strand. Helicase unwinds the helix; primase lays down RNA primers; DNA polymerase adds
nucleotides only in the 5'-to-3' direction. The leading strand is synthesized
continuously; the lagging strand is made in short Okazaki fragments later joined by DNA
ligase.

## Transcription

RNA polymerase binds a promoter and synthesizes a complementary messenger RNA (mRNA)
strand from a DNA template, using uracil (U) in place of thymine. In eukaryotes the
primary transcript is processed: a 5' cap is added, a poly-A tail is added to the 3'
end, and introns are removed by splicing while exons are joined.

## Translation and the genetic code

Ribosomes read mRNA in three-base units called codons. There are 64 possible codons; 61
specify amino acids and 3 are stop codons. The genetic code is degenerate (redundant):
several codons can specify the same amino acid. The start codon is AUG, which codes for
methionine and sets the reading frame. The three stop codons are UAA, UAG, and UGA;
they specify no amino acid and signal the ribosome to terminate. Transfer RNA (tRNA)
molecules carry amino acids and pair their anticodons to mRNA codons.

## Mutations

Point mutations include silent (no amino-acid change), missense (one amino acid
changed), and nonsense (creates a premature stop codon). Insertions or deletions not in
multiples of three cause frameshift mutations that alter all downstream codons.
